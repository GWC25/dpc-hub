General Digital Skills

Drawn from the DfE Essential Digital Skills Framework and the FutureDotNow AI-updated EDS (2025), these skills are organised across three contexts: learning, work, and societal inclusion. The five core EDS categories — Communicating, Handling Information & Content, Transacting, Problem Solving, and Being Safe & Legal Online — cut across all three contexts.

Why this matters at Weston College: The EIF Toolkit explicitly states that learners must develop "the digital skills needed to study curriculum and learning goals." These 15 foundational skills represent the floor — the minimum every learner should reach regardless of their programme.
📚

For Learning

01

Navigating Digital Platforms

Using VLEs, Teams, SharePoint; accessing learning resources and timetables online.

02

Research & Information Literacy

Searching effectively, evaluating sources, avoiding misinformation and AI-generated errors.

03

Digital Note-taking & Organisation

OneNote, cloud documents, folder structures, and version control for assignments.

04

Collaborative Digital Working

Shared documents, group tasks in Teams or Google Workspace, peer review online.

05

AI-Assisted Learning

Using AI tools ethically for research, drafting and self-quizzing without plagiarism.

💼

For the World of Work

01

Professional Communication

Email etiquette, Teams/Zoom calls, digital messaging in workplace contexts.

02

Productivity & Office Applications

Word processing, spreadsheets, presentations and scheduling tools (Microsoft/Google).

03

Digital Transactions & Invoicing

Online payments, basic invoicing apps, managing work-related digital finances.

04

Data Handling & Record-Keeping

Entering, checking and interpreting workplace data; compliant digital record-keeping.

05

AI Literacy for the Workplace

Using AI assistants, understanding outputs critically, prompt skills and ethical use.

🌐

For Societal Inclusion

01

Accessing Public & Government Services

HMRC, Universal Credit, NHS booking, local council portals and digital identity.

02

Online Safety & Privacy

Password management, recognising scams, two-factor authentication, GDPR basics.

Digital Financial Inclusion

Online banking, comparing services, avoiding fraud, energy switching portals.

04

Critical Engagement with Media

Identifying misinformation, understanding algorithmic bias, responsible social media use.

05

Digital Wellbeing & Accessibility

Screen time awareness, assistive technology, designing/using accessible digital content.

Industry-Specific Digital Skills

These are skills that go beyond the general EDS framework — specialist, sector-specific digital competencies that employers expect but that general digital skills qualifications do not cover. These are the skills your curriculum areas need to consciously plan for within their digital hours and programme delivery.

Note for Digital Pedagogy work: When working with Heads of Area, these are the skills to audit first. They are not covered by standard EDS or GCSE IT qualifications — they require deliberate, contextualised teaching within the subject programme.
🔨

Construction & Built Environment

Beyond general digital skills

BIM & CAD Software

Building Information Modelling (Revit, AutoCAD) — designing, reading and annotating digital plans.

Digital Site Management Tools

QR code clock-in, digital site diaries, health & safety reporting apps (e.g. Fieldwire, Procore).

Digital Measuring & Surveying Devices

Laser levels, digital theodolites, drone survey tools — reading and logging outputs digitally.

Digital Invoicing & Materials Ordering

Sage, Xero, or trade-specific ordering portals; raising and tracking purchase orders digitally.

Sustainability & Carbon Calculation Tools

BREEAM digital tools, energy modelling software, materials calculators for green construction.

Framing Digital Skills
for Industry Needs

How you frame digital skills development makes the difference between staff and learners engaging with it or dismissing it. The most effective framing connects a digital skill directly to an industry outcome they already care about. Below are four strategic framing approaches, each with practical examples for Weston College contexts.

Framing Approach 1

The Industry Standard Frame

Frame digital skills as standard professional practice in that sector — not as technology training, but as what industry employers expect every employee to do on day one.

This works particularly well with learners who are resistant to "digital lessons" but respond to employer expectations.

Construction example Instead of: "Today we're going to learn about QR codes"
Say: "Every major contractor now uses digital clock-in. You won't get paid if you can't do this on site."
Framing Approach 2

The Time-Saving Frame

Frame digital skills as tools that save time and reduce hassle in tasks learners will have to do anyway — invoicing, booking, recording, communicating.

Effective for vocational learners who see admin as a burden and are motivated by efficiency.

Hair & Beauty example Instead of: "We're going to learn about booking software"
Say: "Fresha automatically sends reminders and takes deposits — you spend less time chasing no-shows and more time with clients."
Framing Approach 3

The Employability & Earnings Frame

Link digital competence directly to higher pay, better job prospects, or self-employment viability. Use Labour Market Intelligence to make the case concrete.

Most effective in sectors where wage differentials are visible (e.g. construction site manager vs labourer) and self-employment is common (beauty, creative, construction).

Hospitality example Instead of: "Learning to use EPOS"
Say: "Supervisors who can train staff on digital systems earn £4-6k more than floor staff. This is the skill that moves you into management."
Framing Approach 4

The Authentic Context Frame

Teach digital skills inside real vocational tasks rather than as separate lessons. The digital skill is invisible — the learner is just doing their job.

This is the most powerful approach for learners with low digital confidence or prior negative experiences with technology. The learning happens because the task is meaningful, not because it's a "digital lesson."

Construction example Instead of: "Digital skills session today"
Say: "You need to measure this wall, enter the data into this form, and email it to the project manager. That's today's task." (They've just practised digital measuring, data entry, file handling and email — four EDS categories at once.)
Strategic Principle

The Golden Rule of Industry Framing

Never teach the technology. Teach the task that technology enables.

A construction learner doesn't need to learn "how to use an app." They need to be able to clock in correctly, submit a digital site diary, order materials, and read a BIM drawing. The apps are just how they do it. When digital skills are framed this way, they stop being a separate burden and become invisible, purposeful, and motivating — for learners and for the staff teaching them.
