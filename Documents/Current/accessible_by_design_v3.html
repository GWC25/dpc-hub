The case for change

Why digital accessibility matters — right now

Digital exclusion isn't a niche concern. It affects some of our most vulnerable learners every single day — and the tools to address it are already on your laptop.

people in the UK have a disability that affects how they use technology

#1

Who is affected in our classrooms?
🌍
ESOL learners
Students learning in a language that isn't their first face double the cognitive load in every session. Inaccessible materials — dense PDFs, no captions, no translation support — make an already challenging situation exhausting and demoralising.
🧠
SEND learners
Learners with dyslexia, visual impairments, processing differences, limited mobility, or mental health conditions encounter barriers that accessible design removes entirely. A resource that works for them works better for everyone.
👂
d/Deaf and hard of hearing learners
A recorded lecture without captions is inaccessible. A Teams meeting without live captions excludes. These aren't edge cases — and the fix takes seconds to enable.
📱
Learners experiencing device or connectivity poverty
Coastal and rural Weston-super-Mare has real connectivity gaps. Learners accessing materials on low-spec devices or patchy broadband need resources that are lightweight, structured, and simple to navigate.
The FE White Paper assumes all learners have devices, connectivity, and digital confidence. Our context tells a different story. Accessibility isn't an add-on. It's the precondition for learning.
What the research tells us
📈
Accessible design improves outcomes for all learners
Universal Design for Learning (UDL) research consistently shows that designing for those with the greatest access needs raises the floor for the entire cohort. Captions help ESOL learners, SEND learners — and any learner in a noisy environment, revising at home, or processing complex content.
⚡
The tools already exist — and they're free
Every tool in this resource comes with your existing Microsoft 365 Education licence. There is no procurement process, no budget request, no waiting list. The only barrier is awareness — and you're removing it right now by reading this.
Key message from Jisc DigiFest '26: Digital inclusion is now social inclusion. It is infrastructure, not a nice-to-have. Staff capability is the number one lever — which means every teacher who learns these tools is part of the solution.

The philosophy

Accessible by design — not by afterthought

Accessibility is not a feature you add at the end. It is a mindset you bring to the beginning. Here is what that means in practice — and how to make it second nature.

The design principle in one sentence: If you design for the edges — for those with the greatest access needs — you strengthen the experience for everyone in the middle.
The five principles of Accessible by Design
1
Start with access, not content
Before you create any resource, ask: who will use this, and what barriers might they face? A student with dyslexia, a learner with no laptop at home, a new arrival whose English is at A2. Design for them first — and everyone benefits.
2
Structure is accessibility
Using Heading styles in Word, logical slide order in PowerPoint, and consistent folder structures in Teams isn't just tidy — it's what makes documents navigable by screen readers, and cognitively accessible for learners with processing differences. Structure is not bureaucracy. It's care.
3
Never rely on colour alone
Approximately 8% of men have some form of colour blindness. If your resource uses red for "wrong" and green for "right," it is inaccessible. Always use colour plus shape, label, or pattern. Run the Accessibility Checker — it flags contrast issues automatically.
4
Every image needs a text alternative
Alt text — a short description of what an image shows — is what a screen reader reads aloud when a learner with a visual impairment encounters an image. Right-click any image → Edit alt text. Two sentences. Thirty seconds. Transforms access for that learner entirely.
5
Build it in — don't bolt it on
The goal is for accessibility to become as automatic as saving your file. Not a checklist you remember at the end — a habit embedded in how you create. Word over PDF where possible. Heading styles, always. Accessibility Checker before upload, always. Captions on by default. Always.
Your pre-upload accessibility checklist

Use this every time you upload a resource to Teams or Moodle. Tick each item as you go.

Document uses Heading styles (Heading 1, 2, 3) — not just bold or large text Structure
All images have alt text — right-click image → Edit alt text Images
Accessibility Checker has been run and issues resolved — Review tab → Check Accessibility Check
Colour contrast is sufficient — body text is not light grey on white; key information is not conveyed by colour alone Colour
File saved as Word (.docx) where possible — not a scanned PDF Format
If PDF is unavoidable, it has been exported from Word (not scanned) so text is selectable Format
Font is at least 12pt, a readable typeface (e.g. Calibri, Arial, Verdana), and not fully justified Typography
Links use descriptive text — "Download the assignment brief" not "click here" Links
If the resource includes video, it has captions or a text transcript Media
File is saved in the correct Teams folder with a clear, descriptive filename Organisation
